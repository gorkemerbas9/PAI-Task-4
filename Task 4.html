1. Task description

In this task, you will fine-tune a pretrained diffusion policy with reinforcement learning (RL). The policy was trained to imitate a cautious demonstrator that steers a robot around two obstacles to a goal. Your objective is to make the robot reach the goal as fast as possible while minimizing crashes. Imitating the demonstrations alone cannot achieve this.

Task Background

Diffusion policies

Diffusion models generate samples by starting from pure noise and removing it step by step (Ho et al., 2020). A diffusion policy uses the same idea to generate actions: given an observation \( o \), it starts from noise and denoises it into a short sequence of future actions, an action chunk \( a = (a_1, \dotsc, a_H) \) (Chi et al., 2023). Writing \( a^k \) for the chunk after \( K - k \) denoising steps, sampling runs the reverse chain $$ a^K \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad a^{k-1} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mu_\theta(a^k, k, o),\, \sigma_k^2 I\big), \quad k = K, \dotsc, 1, $$ and the robot executes the final chunk \( a^0 \). Here, \( \mu_\theta \) is computed by a neural network (the denoiser) and the noise levels \( \sigma_k \) are fixed. Unlike a Gaussian policy, a diffusion policy can represent action distributions with several modes, such as "go above the obstacle" and "go below the obstacle" (see the figure).

Why imitation is not enough

We provide a diffusion policy \( \mu_{\theta_0} \) that has already been trained on demonstrations, so you do not need to train a diffusion model yourself. However, a policy trained to imitate demonstrations only reproduces the demonstrator, and our demonstrator is cautious. In contrast, RL optimizes for the objective: the policy tries behavior the demonstrator never showed, and learns from the outcome.

A diffusion policy as a decision process

To fine-tune a diffusion policy with policy gradients, we need the probability of the actions it takes. The final chunk \( a^0 \) has no tractable density, but every denoising step is Gaussian. The key idea of DPPO (Ren et al., 2024) is therefore to treat each denoising step as an action of its own, with policy $$ \pi_\theta(a^{k-1} \mid a^k, k, o) = \mathcal{N}\big(a^{k-1};\, \mu_\theta(a^k, k, o),\, \sigma_k^2 I\big). $$ This gives a decision process with two levels (see the diagram).

The log-probability of the whole denoising chain at robot time step \( t \) is the sum of \( K \) Gaussian log-densities, which is all you need for RL algorithms such as PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). In such algorithms, all denoising steps that produced a chunk can share that chunk's advantage, estimated with a value function \( V_\phi(o) \) on the outer level.

Problem Setup

Environment

The robot is a point mass moving in a 2D arena \( [0, 10]^2 \). Its state consists of a position \( p \in \mathbb{R}^2 \) and a velocity \( v \in \mathbb{R}^2 \), and the action is an acceleration \( u \in \mathbb{R}^2 \) with \( \lVert u \rVert \leq 1 \). Its speed is limited to \( 3 \), and the dynamics include a small amount of actuator noise. Every episode takes place in a scene: a start position, a goal position, and the positions and radii of two round obstacles. Concretely:

  • The observation \( o \) contains the robot's position and velocity, the goal position, and each obstacle's center and radius. The obstacles can be larger than observed: in every episode, a hidden amount is added to each radius, distributed as \( 0.15 \lvert \varepsilon \rvert \) with \( \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \).
  • The time step is \( \Delta t = 0.1 \), and an episode lasts at most \( T_{\max} = 150 \) steps.
  • The policy outputs chunks of \( H = 4 \) actions, produced in \( K = 10 \) denoising steps. The whole chunk is executed before the policy is queried again, so one robot time step \( t \) of the outer level consists of \( H \) environment steps.
  • An episode ends when the robot reaches the goal, collides with an obstacle, leaves the arena, or runs out of time. The reward is \( +1 \) for reaching the goal and \( 0 \) otherwise. With the discount factor \( \gamma = 0.99 \), the discounted return is therefore higher the sooner the goal is reached, and zero after a failure.

We refer you to the task4_supplementary.pdf in the handout for more details on the environment.

Pretrained policy

The handout contains the pretrained diffusion policy \( \mu_{\theta_0} \). A scene can be solved in three ways: above both obstacles, below both obstacles, or through the gap between them. The pretrained policy picks one of these routes at random, and is slow but reliable.

Scenes for fine-tuning and evaluation

Pretraining, fine-tuning and evaluation use the same distribution of scenes. The handout provides a fixed set of scenes for fine-tuning, scenes_train.npz. The evaluation scenes come from the same distribution, but are different scenes.

Your Task

Fine-tune the pretrained diffusion policy with RL so that the robot reaches the goal faster. The template was designed for DPPO, but any fine-tuning method is accepted. All sub-tasks are implemented by filling in the solution template solution.py. The concrete sub-tasks are as follows:

  1. Implement sampling that records the whole denoising chain of every chunk, together with the log-probability of every denoising step under your policy. Comments beginning with TODO(1) guide you through this.
  2. Implement a value function \( V_\phi(o) \) and generalized advantage estimation (GAE) (Schulman et al., 2016) over robot time steps. See TODO(2).
  3. Implement the clipped PPO objective over all pairs of robot time step and denoising step, using the advantages from step 2. See TODO(3).
  4. Improve your score. If you are using the provided template designed for DPPO, possible approaches are fine-tuning only the last denoising steps, a lower bound on the noise level \( \sigma_k \) during training for more exploration, and tweaking the PPO hyperparameters. Comments beginning with TODO(4) in the solution template provide pointers for possible extensions.

Important: we designed this task such that you can beat the baseline in about 30 minutes on a laptop CPU, the template's training time, after improving its default settings; no GPU is needed. Using hand-designed controllers, planners, or similar tools may invalidate your submission retroactively. The checker only accepts policies that save_policy in utils.py can save: the provided denoiser architecture, optionally combined with the frozen pretrained denoiser for the early denoising steps (FrozenEarly).

Note that the template in solution.py uses PyTorch. If you are new to PyTorch, you might want to check out the PyTorch quickstart guide.

We refer you to the task4_supplementary.pdf in the handout for more details on the template.

Metrics

Your score is the average discounted return of your fine-tuned policy over \( N = 2000 \) evaluation episodes, $$ \text{score} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma^{\,n_i - 1} \, \mathbb{1}\{\text{episode } i \text{ reaches the goal}\}, \qquad \gamma = 0.99, $$ where \( n_i \) is the number of environment steps of episode \( i \). This is the return defined by the reward above, so a failed episode scores zero. Higher is better. Which route your policy takes does not enter the score directly; only the time it needs and whether it arrives count.

The checker computes a public score and a private score on two hidden sets of scenes. You pass if your public score is at least \( 0.600 \), the score of our baseline. The pretrained policy scores \( 0.417 \) on the same scenes. The private score is used for the private leaderboard.

The checker evaluates your policy in the same way on a third set of scenes, scenes_local.npz, and reports your local score, the fraction of failed episodes, and the average time to goal. The hidden scenes are drawn from the same distribution, so your local score should be a close estimate of your public score.

Submission Workflow

  1. Install and start Docker. Understanding how Docker works and how to use it is beyond the scope of the project. Nevertheless, if you are interested, you could read about its use cases.
  2. Download handout
  3. The handout contains the solution template solution.py. Sections for your implementation are marked with # TODO. Please make sure that your implementation preserves the signature of the fine_tune function, which the checker calls. However, you are free to introduce new functions and classes.
  4. You should use Python 3.8. You are free to use any other libraries that are not already imported in the solution template. Important: Please make sure that you list all additional libraries together with their versions in the requirements.txt file provided in the handout.
  5. Once you have implemented your solution, run the checker in Docker:
    • On Linux, run bash runner.sh. In some cases, you might need to enable Docker for your user if you see a Docker permission denied error. Please also first refer to the provided guide fix-permission-errors.md before posting questions to the forum.
    • On macOS, run bash runner.sh. Docker might by default restrict how much memory your solution may use. Running over the memory limit will result in Docker writing "Killed" to the terminal. If you encounter out-of-memory issues, you can increase the limits as described in the Docker Desktop for Mac user manual. You could alternatively run your solution on the ETH Euler cluster. Please follow the guide specified in euler-guide.md in the handout.
    • On Windows, open a PowerShell, change the directory to the handout folder, and run docker build --tag task4 .; docker run --rm -v "$(pwd):/results" task4.
  6. The checker runs your training code, saves your fine-tuned weights to finetuned_policy.safetensors, prints your local score, and generates a results_check.byte file that contains your scores in encrypted form. If the checker fails, it will display an appropriate error message. To check weights you have already trained without training again, run bash runner.sh python -u checker_client.py --eval-only.
  7. The results_check.byte file constitutes your submission and needs to be uploaded to the project server along with your solution.py and a link to a one-minute video to pass this task. The server reads your scores from this file and shows your public score on the leaderboard.
  8. We limit submissions to the server to 40 per team, with at most 20 in a 24-hour period.

Extended Evaluation

This part of the task is optional but highly encouraged. If you set the global variable ENABLE_EXTENDED_ANALYSIS in the solution script to True, the checker saves a PDF that plots trajectories of the pretrained policy next to trajectories of your fine-tuned policy on the same episodes, and reports which routes each of them takes. This is the quickest way to see whether your policy has become faster, and whether it adapts its route to the scene.
